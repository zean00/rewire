Act I — the spark

A new kind of AI showed up. Everyone copied it.
We wired it in.

In 2026, a lab called TypeSafe published System One Models & Jev, borrowing a page from psychology: human thinking runs on two systems — a fast, automatic System 1 (spot the danger, grab the railing) and a slow, deliberate System 2 (write the essay, plan the trip).

AI, they argued, has superb System 2 — the LLM, the engine behind ChatGPT — and almost no System 1. So they built Jev: an AI that doesn't write at all. It takes a situation and returns a typed decision — a choice, a score, a route — in tens to hundreds of milliseconds, with a calibrated confidence attached. Because it never strings words together, it cannot invent things. TypeSafe claims it is 70–500 ms end-to-end where frontier LLMs take 3–329 s — headline numbers like 193.6× faster and 444.6× cheaper on workflow tasks.

what the crowd built

Replicas and rivals

An open-source wave followed Jev — and almost all of it raced down the same road: recreate or compete with Jev. Diffusion-based decision servers (the djev project on vLLM's System One server, PR #57250), re-implementations, benchmark suites. A new model category, chasing the original.

the rewire bet

Complements, not competitors

We read Jev differently. A reflex that only points and a mind that can write are not rivals — they're a pair. The reflex is fast and grounded; the LLM can think, see images, and generate text. So Rewire doesn't rebuild Jev. It plugs Jev-style reflexes into a full LLM — and, the twist: without training anything new.

Why "Rewire"? Because nothing inside the model changes. We don't touch the weights, we don't modify the inference engine. We insert one layer between the engine and its OpenAI-compatible API — the same interface every tool already speaks. From the outside, your agent still talks to "a model." From the inside, decisions now take the express lane. Same brain; new wiring.
For the engineers — Jev's claims vs what Rewire actually set out to test

Jev: unstructured state + a bounded rubric in → typed probabilistic decision out (noul/choice/score), schema-guaranteed, calibrated confidence, 70–500 ms, RLCD-style training, parallel sampler, $0.042/MTok. Their workflow evals show them owning the Pareto frontier vs top external LLMs (with their own workflows as a caveat).

Rewire deliberately tests a different thesis: that the same fast/grounded behavior can be read out of a frozen general LLM at the logit level, gated, and composed with generation — no new architecture, no training run. If that works even partly, the classifier and the LLM stop being two products and become one endpoint. The PoC result, in one line: it works exactly where the task hands the read a real signal, and we measured precisely where that ends.

Act II — for everyone

Two brains, one wire

🧭 if you use ChatGPT, you already know one of them

Here's the secret of how ChatGPT-style models work: they are word-prediction machines. To write a sentence, they predict the next word — then the next — thousands of times. And here's the part almost nobody knows: before each word, the model holds a giant probability list of every word it could possibly say next. That list is the model's raw, unfiltered "gut feeling."

Rewire's core trick: when a task is a choice, we read the gut feeling directly and skip the writing entirely. No essay. No "Sure! Here's the answer…". Just: look at the probability list over the options, take the answer, done. One look instead of a thousand words.

brain 1 — the writer

THINK (the LLM)

Can plan, reason, see screenshots, and write anything. Magic for open-ended work.

Weakness: slow (it literally writes every answer word by word), and because it writes, it can write things that sound right but aren't — hallucination.

brain 2 — the reflex

DECIDE (the read)

Doesn't write. Looks at the internal probability list over a list of options and points at one. One glance: ~0.26 seconds, often more accurate for choices, and it cannot make things up — it can only pick from options you listed.

Weakness: it can only answer questions you can phrase as picking from a list.

the referee

GATE (knowing when it doesn't know)

The reflex reports a confidence. When confidence is low, GATE doesn't guess — it escalates to thinking, or asks a human. In live web tests: asked a human on 10% of decisions, and every one of those asks was genuinely one a human should see (precision 1.0).

"Can't it still be wrong?" Yes — and we never claim otherwise. Wrong ≠ invented. When a person points at the wrong button, they didn't hallucinate; they chose badly. The reflex has the same honesty: its answer is always one of the real options on the screen, never a fabricated one. For automation, that difference is everything: a wrong pick can be checked in a blink; an invented answer poisons everything downstream.

Try it: which brain gets the job?

Three everyday calls an AI assistant might face. Pick the brain you'd assign — Rewire's measured answer flips open.

🌤️"Which of these six weather apps should I open?"
Rewire agrees: point.
Choosing among named options is exactly what the read is for. Measured on 500 real questions: pointing got 85.6% right in about a quarter of a second; forcing the model to think in words first got 49.0% — and took 17.29 s. For choices, writing isn't just slow. It's noise.
🥂"Write a wedding toast for Priya and Sam."
Rewire agrees: write.
There is no list to point at — this is creation, the writer's home turf. This is why Rewire is a wiring and not a replacement: the reflex handles the choices, and full generation stays one routing decision away. In the hybrid, only the ~18% of items that genuinely need it get escalated to thinking at all.
🖱️"This page has 41 buttons. Which one submits the form?"
Rewire's answer: point — and let the gate watch.
The reflex scores all 41 candidates in one pass (batched reads cut this from 1.70 s to 0.34 s). On live staged websites it picked the right element 85% of the time (90% after human adjudication) — and crucially, its two mistakes were exactly its two lowest-confidence calls. The gate caught both. Low confidence → ask a human: 10% of decisions, every ask legitimate.

The whole integration

Same machine, same model file, same agent harness, same prompts. The layer speaks the exact OpenAI-compatible protocol, so to every existing tool it's still "just a model" — one that happens to have reflexes now. The live numbers are five real agent tasks, run blind, on two different models (4B: 0/5 → 5/5; 12B: 0/5 → 4/5).

For the engineers — what the read actually reads

The layer issues a forced-choice prompt that ends at the first token of each candidate, and reads top-k logprobs from the engine's standard API — no sampling, no decode steps. Candidate scores are normalized into a bounded rubric score E ∈ [0,4] over four levels (far / partly / nearly / fully). The gate is a temperature-scaled fitted threshold on that score, not a vibe. Batched candidate reads plus a bit-parity check cut p50 read latency 5× (1.70 → 0.34 s) with 98.3% agreement vs sequential reads.

One engine-side discovery mattered: under a grammar-constrained read, this llama.cpp build ignores sampling temperature — logprobs came back byte-identical at T = 0.0 / 0.7 / 1.5. Reads are order-stable but framing-fragile, which becomes a lead actor later in this story. Also: reads required -np 1 --no-cache-prompt to be bit-exact — a silent KV-cache corruption under batching was found and pinned with byte-level diffs.

Routing & speed

Same model, same questions, four ways of asking. "Escalate → re-decide" = let the reflex answer, and for the 18% it flags as uncertain, let the model think, then re-ask the reflex. p50 latencies in the labels.

The surprise: "think always" — the setting people assume is smartest — was the worst (49.0%) and sixty times slower. Deliberation is not free, and on multiple-choice work it's a tax with no return. The reflex-first hybrid beat both extremes.

Replayed web-agent steps: given the page, pick the next browser action. Correctness = exact action match.

The win: rewire vs no-think: +9.8 points, p ≈ 8×10⁻⁹ — and vs think-always, a chasm (11.5% at 27.4 s per step). The reflex's edge survives when decisions get hard.

Same, for structured tool calls (choose the operation + arguments). "Unparseable" = the model's own JSON didn't even parse.

The win: think-always produced broken JSON on 79% of items; the one-shot no-think model collapsed into clicking the same button 896/900 times. The wired chain fixed both failure modes: +4.6 points over one-shot (p = 0.0033), and operation-choice accuracy rose from 50.7% → 84.3%.
latency

Batching the reflex: 5× faster

Scoring 41 candidates one-by-one: p50 1.70 s. Batched into single passes: 0.34 s — with 98.3% agreement. That's what makes 41-button pages decidable in real time.

Live staged sites, end to end: p50 0.75 s per agent step, element accuracy 0.850 (0.90 adjudicated), action effects 0.950, and value extraction 9/9.

the honest cell

Routing by feel: no

We then asked reads a harder question: "which mode of the model will succeed?" They routed worse than always guessing (0.834 vs 0.844) — and even an oracle tuner loses 2.8 points. The signal isn't hiding; it isn't there.

That null became the most valuable number in the project. Keep scrolling.

The journey, in eight eras

Era 1 · the router

Discovery: reading beats asking

500-question shoot-out. Reflex-with-escape-hatch 0.856 @ 0.26 s; think-always 0.490 @ 17.29 s. The thesis is born.

Era 2 · web actions

The reflex generalizes to real pages

Gated re-read chain 0.356 vs 0.258 no-think (p ≈ 8×10⁻⁹). Batched reads make it 5× faster.

Era 3 · tool calls

Structured output, no structure tax

Chain 0.320 vs 0.274 one-shot (p = 0.0033) — while think-always drowned in its own unparseable JSON.

Era 4 · live

From replay to reality

Staged live sites: 0.85–0.95 across metrics; the gate ranks its own mistakes perfectly. Then the real harness: vanilla 0/5 → rewired 5/5 on the same model.

Era 5 · the verifier

We pre-registered a gate for the model to check its own work. It failed. On schedule.

168 agent episodes, 316 frozen probes across three one-pass experiments (binary, rubric, K=5 paraphrase framings). Verdict: reads carry no outcome signal. Falsified cleanly, by design.

Era 6 · the forensics

Why the hardest benchmark tied — down to the exact line

Autopsy of 147 failed episodes: 55 ended while the scoreline "Last reward: −1" was visibly on screen. The failure isn't reasoning; it's reward-blindness. A one-line regex gate catches all 55 — with zero false stops.

Era 7 · the verdict

The chain earns its complexity where signal exists — and says so where it doesn't

Strategic decision recorded, with a written bar for revisiting every suspended piece: AUROC ≥ 0.75 AND Brier under the constant, on fresh sessions. See the verdict below.

Era 8 · the reversal

Inspired by a public verifier (CLM), the state-vector dial cleared the frozen bar — live

Five read families had failed the bar. The sixth — a tiny calibrated head over frozen encoder state vectors — went 0.9889 on a second encoder, 0.8867 on the chain's own backbone, then 0.9011 on 168 brand-new sessions it had never seen. GATE PASSED. The dial ships. The full ladder ↓

12B model

0/5 → 4/5

Bigger model, same story — bigger brains alone didn't finish these tasks either (0/5 raw). The wiring, not the size, was the missing part.

the gate

It knows what it doesn't know

On live sites, the two decisions the reflex got wrong were exactly its two lowest-confidence calls (~0.4 threshold). With an ask-a-human rule: 10% ask rate, precision 1.0.

Then the hardest benchmark — and the honest tie

MiniWoB++: 96 full agent episodes, 48 per arm, chain vs vanilla. Final score: 11/48 vs 10/48 — statistically a tie, at 2.1× the wall-clock. A lesser report would have buried this. Instead we took it apart.

what the tie hid — both halves

The reflex behaved; the errors turned confident

The good half: on 79% of its reads (283/359) the reflex stood down and let generation answer — exactly the division of labor Rewire exists for.

The cost, stated plainly: the chain's mistakes were confident and terminal — 29 wrong-action failures vs vanilla's 18 inert stumbles. On short-horizon tasks where one wrong click ends the game, there is no room to pay for the layer. That's a statement about the benchmark's structure, not a win for anyone.

what actually broke

The agent couldn't see the scoreline

Across 147 failed episodes, the single dominant failure: the agent ends the episode while the text "Last reward: −1" is visibly in its context. It claims success on a failed page — 55 such blind finishes. Not a reasoning gap. A perception gap at the harness floor.

The cheapest winning move in the whole project: a one-line regular expression — Last reward: -\d — that vetoes any "I'm done" while a failing scoreline is on screen. Counterfactual replay: catches all 55 blind finishes, redirects ~30 more wandering episodes, and fires zero false stops on the 21 genuine passes. A regex beat every learned verifier we tested. Sometimes the right wire is copper.
The honest part

What didn't work — and why we led with it

Every claim above came with a pre-registered pass/fail bar written before the data ran. Five designs died at their own gates. We consider that list the proof of quality of everything that survived.

falsified

Self-verification reads

Can the reflex tell a passing episode from a failing one? No. Across 316 probes: detecting a pass at 88.8% cost 83.3% false alarms. Calibration: worse than a coin that always says "fail" (AUROC 0.51–0.57; high-confidence tail: predicted ≥80% → 16.7% actual; ≥90% → 0%).

True — for reads over the model's words. Two days later the same written bar was cleared by reading its state vectors instead. The dial ↓

falsified

Paraphrase armor doesn't help

Hypothesis: reads fail on outcomes but are robust if phrasing is frozen. K=5 pre-registered paraphrases: canonical phrasing reproduced prior results bit-for-bit (316/316) — and the gate still failed its bar (3/28 confirm, 32/288 false-approve). Rewording moves a read ~1.5 rubric points; pass/fail differs by 0.15–0.4. The read measures the sentence, not the page.

dead end

The public benchmark wasn't public

We tried to calibrate against Jev's public suite (JevBench, N=231). It doesn't exist publicly: no data, no harness, no DNS, no leaderboard. We substituted an equivalent calibration on our own stack rather than pretend — every read-based verifier scored below a constant predictor.

falsified

Read-order tricks

Sequential candidate reads looked +13.7% better — but failed their pre-registered 97% agreement bar (72.7%). Reorder-robustness: FAIL. Positional primacy: −0.068. The early wins were ordering artifacts; the batched path is the honest one.

falsified

Jev-style sidecar, 82.9%

Our first Jev-replica attempt scored 82.9% on the sidecar benchmark — respectable, until audit day: the constant predictor scores 82.9%. Killed at its own gate. This is what pre-registration is for.

caught & fixed

Silent read corruption

Batched reads were returning subtly corrupted logprobs — caught by a bit-parity check, root-caused to KV-cache reuse, pinned byte-exact with -np 1 --no-cache-prompt, and re-verified. Every read number on this page is from the fixed path.

The rules the whole project obeyed

pre-register framing, evidence & thresholds before data one pass per instrument — no reruns until significance sanity gates allowed to kill designs (and did, 5×) bit-exact reproduction checks (316/316, sha256-pinned) every deviation disclosed in the ledger blind adjudication from transcripts, never exit codes

Full audit trail: a 1,600-line experiment ledger (IMPLEMENTATION_PLAN.md), the raw report (poc_report.html), and the structured conclusion (POC_CONCLUSION.md) — all in the repo.

Act IV — the plot twist

Then the bar that killed every read was cleared

Two days after the verdict, a public project reframed the question. Contrastive-LM's CLM verifies agents with a frozen LLM encoder and tiny trained heads — matching state vectors, never asking the model to write a verdict. We imported the idea, not the checkpoint, and pointed it at this project's frozen bar: AUROC ≥ 0.75 AND Brier under the constant predictor, pre-registered, one pass, on fresh sessions.

what failed — five times

Asking the model in words

"Is this episode passing?" — as a prompt, a rubric, or a trained contrastive head fed by phrasing-sensitive margins. Words can be talked into things: rewording moved the reads ~10× more than the outcomes did. The fifth family (CLM's own heads) ranked brilliantly and calibrated terribly — the conjunction gate caught it, exactly as designed.

what passed

Reading where its state lands

At the moment the agent claims "done", embed the evidence block it just acted on — verbatim, no question attached — through the engine's stock embeddings API, and score where that thought landed: p = σ(a·(w·x+b)+c). 3,840 weights, two Platt scalars, one threshold. No second model. No sklearn in the serving path. One dot product.

What the dial does at its frozen operating point (140 fresh completion claims): when it accepts a "done" claim it is right 93.3% of the time, and it catches 113 of 114 false completion claims — 99.1%. For the engineers — the readout, the instrument rules, and what entry M settled

Readout. Last-token-pooled, L2-normalized 3,840-d embedding of the completion-claim evidence block (goal lines + page snapshot — the same block the rubric reviewer read), scored by a logistic head plus Platt map exported as pure JSON data (3,840 weights + b + a + c + τ = 0.3261). Serve cost ≈ 95 ms per claim through the engine's stock /v1/embeddings — the dial rides the serving engine itself.

Instrument rules, learned the hard way. Batched multi-input embedding requests on this server build return position-dependent vectors (the same text alone vs inside a batch differs by up to cos 0.87) — so the standing rule is one text per request, strictly serial. And the repeat-request determinism needed for entry M's attribution took three stacked fixes in a local diagnostic build (slot LCP KV keep, CUDA-graph capture-vs-replay, allocator growth churn), each diagnosed to source level, fixed, and revalidated bit-exact before any metric existed. The shipped dial needs none of that machinery — it just follows the one-text-per-request rule.

Entry M. Qwen3-8B at ~4.8 bits through the same serving stack lands within noise of bf16 (0.9868 vs 0.9889 AUROC): weight precision and the numerics stack are exonerated — the gap between 0.89 (Gemma's vectors) and 0.99 (Qwen's) is the backbone's own representation. Any stronger open-weights encoder can now drop in behind the same recipe and the same frozen bar.

Act V — the verdict
"The reflex works wherever the task hands it a real signal — choices, candidates, rankings — and it delivers those wins at 1/60th the latency and zero hallucination surface. Asked to divine things it was never shown, it fails — and now we know exactly where that line sits, to the decimal. And when the line mattered most, we found the way across it: not in the model's words, but in its state."
— the recorded verdict, Sep 2026

So: does the chain earn its complexity?

Yes — in its current trimmed form, conditionally. The strategic review weighed every component against its measured contribution. What stays, what gets wired, what waits:

keep & wire now

Mechanical gates first

The reward-line regex and its siblings: deterministic, free, and already proven to beat every learned verifier. Wire them into the harness floor before spending a single model read.

keep

Decide + gate as shipped

Candidate reads, batched and parity-checked; calibrated thresholds; the ask-a-human ranking. This is the part that turned 0/5 into 5/5.

decisive test next

The A/B that settles it

Rewired chain + mechanical gates vs vanilla + prompt fixes alone, same tasks, pre-registered. If vanilla with good prompts matches the chain, the chain must shrink. We suspect it won't — but the experiment, not the suspicion, decides.

bar written → bar met

Self-verification, by a different instrument

The written bar — AUROC ≥ 0.75 and Brier better than the constant predictor — killed five read families. The state-vector dial cleared it on fresh sessions (0.9011 / 0.0861 vs 0.1512) and now ships as the GATE's completion-claim verifier. The discipline never bent; the instrument changed.

the big one

Native decide, inside the architecture

Today the reflex borrows a next-word probability list that was trained to write. Native decide means training decision heads into the backbone itself: typed outputs (choice / score / route / abstain) with calibrated confidence as a first-class training objective — Jev's RLCD insight, but sharing one body with the writer instead of shipping a second model.

The PoC is the flight checklist for that future: declare the read's contract (reads worked when told exactly what to score — the oracle result shows undeclared jobs fail), refit calibration per model (our numbers never ported between 4B and 12B), and keep the acceptance bar: AUROC ≥ 0.75 + Brier under the constant predictor, or the head doesn't ship.

already half-proven

Multimodal decide

Our screenshot-conditioned reads matched DOM-based reads (0.960 agreement-level performance) through the existing API. The reflex doesn't need text — it needs signal. Native vision-decide heads are the natural next step, and browser agents are the killing application.

cheapest lesson, biggest ROI

Mechanical-first gating

The regex beat the neurons. Generalize it: an inventory of deterministic harness signals (scorelines, timers, error states) wired as hard gates — with model reads spent only on the residual they can't see.

next wire

Enforce mode

Today the dial verifies; next it steers. A rejected completion claim re-opens the episode — with the reject policy, retry cap, and escalation path frozen in a pre-registration before it goes live. The harness-floor regex and the dial become one gate stack.

measured headroom

Stronger encoders, same recipe

Entry M showed the dial is backbone-agnostic and quantization-proof (Q4 ≈ bf16). The gap between 0.89 (Gemma's vectors) and 0.99 (Qwen's) is representation — and representation is swappable. Any stronger open-weights encoder drops in behind the same frozen bar.

a standard worth existing

The Rewire contract

The layer already speaks plain OpenAI-compatible HTTP. The endpoint could become a spec: /v1/decide — candidates in, typed decision + calibrated confidence out, abstain as a legal answer. Any harness, any engine, one wire.
